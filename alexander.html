Ancient & Classical · 356–323 BCE

Alexander the Great

King of Macedon, Pharaoh of Egypt, King of Persia — the conqueror who never lost a battle and created the largest empire the ancient world had ever known.

Biography

Rise of Alexander

Alexander III of Macedon (356–323 BCE), commonly known as Alexander the Great, was the son of King Philip II of Macedon and Olympias, a princess of Epirus. Tutored by the philosopher Aristotle from the age of 13, Alexander developed a lifelong passion for learning alongside his exceptional military instincts.

Philip II had transformed Macedonia from a minor kingdom into the dominant military power in Greece, creating the formidable Macedonian army with its revolutionary phalanx formation armed with 5.5-metre sarissa pikes. When Philip was assassinated in 336 BCE, Alexander inherited both the throne and his father's superbly trained army at the age of 20.

After consolidating his power in Greece and crushing revolts in Thebes, Alexander launched his campaign against the vast Persian Empire in 334 BCE with approximately 40,000 troops. Over the next eleven years, he would never lose a single battle, conquering territories stretching from Egypt to the borders of India.

Military Organisation

The Macedonian Army

Alexander's army was the most sophisticated military force of the ancient world, built on innovations pioneered by his father Philip II:

The Phalanx (Pezhetairoi)

Heavy infantry armed with the sarissa — a 5.5-metre pike — and small shields. Arranged in formations 16 ranks deep, the phalanx presented an impenetrable wall of pike points. The sarissa outreached any contemporary weapon, giving the phalanx a decisive advantage in frontal engagements.

Companion Cavalry (Hetairoi)

The elite shock cavalry of the Macedonian army, personally led by Alexander in battle. Armed with the xyston lance and trained for wedge-formation charges, the Companions were the decisive striking force. Alexander typically used them to exploit gaps in enemy lines created by the phalanx's pressure.

Hypaspists (Shield Bearers)

Elite heavy infantry more mobile than the phalanx, used as a flexible link between the phalanx and cavalry. They could fight in looser formations and were often used for rapid assaults, night attacks and difficult terrain operations.

Combined Arms

Alexander pioneered the systematic integration of heavy infantry, shock cavalry, light troops (peltasts, archers, slingers), and siege engineers into a unified tactical system — an approach that would not be matched for centuries.

Campaigns

The Conquest of the Known World

Persian Campaign (334–330 BCE)

Alexander crossed the Hellespont into Asia Minor in 334 BCE with approximately 40,000 troops. His first major victory at the Battle of the Granicus opened Asia Minor. At Issus in 333 BCE, he defeated Darius III for the first time. After the epic seven-month Siege of Tyre, he conquered Egypt (founding the city of Alexandria), then delivered the decisive blow at Gaugamela in 331 BCE, ending the Persian Empire forever.

Central Asian Campaign (330–327 BCE)

After Gaugamela, Alexander pursued Darius into the eastern provinces. In Bactria and Sogdiana (modern Afghanistan and Central Asia), he faced a protracted guerrilla war — the most difficult phase of his campaigns. He eventually pacified the region through a combination of military force and diplomatic marriages.

Indian Campaign (327–325 BCE)

Alexander invaded the Indian subcontinent in 327 BCE. At the Battle of the Hydaspes (326 BCE), he defeated King Porus and his war elephants in what would be his last major battle. His exhausted army mutinied at the Hyphasis River, refusing to march further east. Alexander was forced to turn back, reaching Babylon in 323 BCE where he died, aged 32, possibly from fever, poison, or the cumulative effects of his many battle wounds.

Legacy

Historical Significance

Alexander's conquests spread Greek culture, language and ideas across a vast area from the Mediterranean to Central Asia, inaugurating the Hellenistic period. The cities he founded — particularly Alexandria in Egypt — became major centres of learning and trade. His military innovations influenced warfare for centuries, and his campaigns remain foundational case studies in military academies worldwide.

His empire, however, did not survive his death. Without a clear successor, it fragmented among his generals (the Diadochi) into several competing kingdoms, including Ptolemaic Egypt, the Seleucid Empire, and Antigonid Macedonia.
